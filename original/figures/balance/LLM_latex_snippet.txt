The dataset comprises $N=74,771$ samples of the closest vessel-to-fiber distance, ranging from 13.35~m to 9127.73~m. The overall distribution is strongly right-skewed, evidenced by a mean of 2135.63~m that significantly exceeds the median of 1722.43~m. This skewness indicates that while most vessel passages are clustered at closer ranges (75\% of data is below 3118.25~m ), the distribution has a long tail of distant events.

A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed the data is not normally distributed ($p < 0.001$). Subsequent distribution fitting analysis revealed that no single parametric distribution (e.g., Half-Normal, Weibull, Gamma) provided an adequate fit, with all p-values being effectively zero.

This apparent complexity is explained by significant inter-class variability. The dataset is not a single distribution but a composite mixture of 14 unique ship types, each with distinct operational behaviors. For example, high-frequency commercial vessels like Large Cargo Ships and Tankers navigate at closer median distances (1446.01~m and 1510.48~m, respectively). In stark contrast, other types, such as Research Vessels, operate at significantly greater median distances (5239.56~m). This heterogeneity, not a single stochastic process, defines the dataset's structure.

This composite, heavy-tailed structure has direct implications for machine learning. For regression tasks, predicting the raw distance is ill-advised; a variance-stabilizing transformation, such as predicting $\log(\text{distance})$, is essential. For classification at a given threshold (e.g., 500~m), models must be robust to the inherent class imbalance (17.74\% positive class). Given its high predictive power, \texttt{ship\_type} must be included as a primary feature, and model validation should use stratification by type to ensure robust generalization across all vessel behaviors.
